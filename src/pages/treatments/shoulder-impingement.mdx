## **Summary**

Shoulder impingement is one of the leading causes of shoulder complaints[[18](http://dx.doi.org/10.2340/16501977-0453 "Effects of physiotherapy in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: A systematic review of the literature"), [19](http://dx.doi.org/10.1093/rheumatology/keh598 "Diagnosis and relation to general health of shoulder disorders presenting to primary care"), [20](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/8546527 "Shoulder disorders in general practice: incidence, patient characteristics, and management")]. It is defined to be a form of disabling contact between soft-tissue and/or bony structures in the shoulder complex. Subacromial impingement, internal impingement, coracoid impingement and suprascapular nerve entrapment are the four types of shoulder impingement.

There are many signs and symptoms of impingement. The two main ones are pain and reduced range of motion or strength.

There are multiple theories of causes, which can also be a result of impingement. This has made defining treatment methods difficult. However numerous investigations in both conservative and non-conservative treatment methods suggest that physiotherapy is currently the best go to treatment option, whilst surgery and injections may not provide any additional benefit.

Literature recommends individualised neuromuscular control training or PNF, eccentric training and scapulothoracic strengthening regimens to best tackle shoulder impingement. We have developed our shoulder impingement treatment plan based on combining these recommendations with our powerful, smart MSK shoulder devices.

## **What is Shoulder Impingement?**

The term “Impingement” can be defined to be some form of a disabling contact between the soft-tissues and bony structures within the shoulder complex. This could be contact between the following:

- Soft-tissue sandwiched and compressed between bony structures, like a pinch (Bone – Soft-tissue – Bone).
- Grinding between soft-tissue and bony structures (Soft-tissue – Bone).
- Collision between bony structures (Bone – Bone).

This disabling contact is present during different motions of the upper arm. Various types of impingement have been defined in clinical research and they are as follows[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes")]:

1. Subacromial impingement Syndrome – formerly known as supraspinatus syndrome.
2. Internal impingement – Also referred to as the posterosuperior glenoid rim impingement.
3. Coracoid impingement.
4. Suprascapular nerve impingement.

It is important to note here that this definition of impingement does not include contact with a joint replacement or implant. For purposes of this article, we have not included this type of impingement.

A brief history of shoulder impingement will be described followed by each of the individual types of shoulder impingements defined above.

## **History of Shoulder Impingement**

Shoulder impingement was first reported and described in 1852[[2](http://www.mujofitness.com/ShoulderImpingement#ref2 "Shoulder Joint. In T. Bentley (Ed.), Cyclopedia of anatomy and physiology")]. It was later termed “supraspinatus syndrome” throughout the mid 1900s[[3](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/18148779 " Excision of the acromion in treatment of the supraspinatus syndrome; report of 95 excisions"), [4](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Abstract/1962/44030/Complete_Acromionectomy_in_the_Treatment_of.7.aspx "Complete Acromionectomy in the Treatment of Chronic Tendinit...")]. At the time, supraspinatus syndrome was initially thought to occur when the supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa, were compressed and sandwiched between the humeral head and the lateral component of the acromion. However, Charles Neer II argued and showed that it was compression on the anterior portion of the acromion in 1972[[5](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Abstract/1972/54010/Anterior_Acromioplasty_for_the_Chronic_Impingement.3.aspx "Anterior Acromioplasty for the Chronic Impingement Syndrome...")]. The term “supraspinatus syndrome” was then dropped and replaced by the term “shoulder impingement syndrome”. This became well-established and the definition still stands today although with a minor refinement in 1990[[6](http://www.mujofitness.com/ShoulderImpingement#ref6 "The Shoulder")]. Shoulder impingement syndrome as described by Neer is what is known as “**Subacromial Impingement**” today.

The second type of shoulder impingement, “**Internal Impingement**” was reported and described by Walch in 1992[[7](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(09)80065-7 "Impingement of the deep surface of the supraspinatus tendon on the posterosuperior glenoid rim: An arthroscopic study")]. The third type of impingement, “**Coracoid Impingement**” was first reported and described by Goldthwait in 1909[[8](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Citation/1909/06040/AN_ANATOMIC_AND_MECHANICAL_STUDY_OF_THE.1.aspx "AN ANATOMIC AND MECHANICAL STUDY OF THE SHOULDER-JOINT, EXPL...")], before supraspinatus syndrome was defined and studied. Finally, “**Suprascapular Nerve Impingement**” was initially reported and described by Thomas in 1936[[9](http://www.mujofitness.com/ShoulderImpingement#ref9 "La paralyse du muscle susepineux")].

The timeline of discovery of the different types of impingement and their refinements show that this is a complex disease and one that is difficult to study. This is still the case today as there are differing opinions about causes of impingement[[10](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.physio.2015.03.029 "Shoulder impingement syndrome: how does opinion regarding aetiology influence treatment?")] and how different clinicians and researchers define the terms[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy")].

## **The different Types of Shoulder Impingement**

The four different types of impingement are described in detail here.

### **Subacromial Impingement**

This type of impingement is probably the most studied disease in comparison to the other types.

#### **What is Subacromial Impingement?**

Currently, it is defined to be the “compression of supraspinatus tendon, subacromial bursa with the anterior third of the acromion, coracoid, coracoacromial ligament and the AC joint”.

Charles Neer further categorised the progression of the disease into three stages[[12](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/6825348 "Impingement lesions"), [13](http://dx.doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S36646 "Optimal management of shoulder impingement syndrome")]. Each stage was found to be present in certain age groups. They are as follows:

1. Under 25-: Aching discomfort in supraspinatus tendon and long head of bicep. Histologically, there is edema and haemorrhage of the supraspinatus and a synovial reaction in the subacromial bursa making it larger.
2. 25 – 40-: The continued irritation and impingement leads to fibrotic changes in the supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa, further reducing the subacromial space. This causes pain during activity.
3. Above 40-: Extended history of shoulder pain and continued impingement leads to development of osteophytes and partial or full thickness rotator cuff tears.

#### **When does Subacromial Impingement Occur?**

It is thought that some motions are far more likely to result in impingement when a patient suffers from it. Neer claimed that a humeral elevation at 90° with internal rotation results in impingement as it reduces the subacromial space[[5](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Abstract/1972/54010/Anterior_Acromioplasty_for_the_Chronic_Impingement.3.aspx "Anterior Acromioplasty for the Chronic Impingement Syndrome...")]. In this position the scapula has not rotated upward sufficiently[[14](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10696154 "Alterations in shoulder kinematics and associated muscle activity in people with symptoms of shoulder impingement"), [15](http://dx.doi.org/10.2519/jospt.1999.29.10.574 "Comparison of 3-Dimensional Scapular Position and Orientation Between Subjects With and Without Shoulder Impingement")]. In addition, a general lack of upward rotation and posterior tilt of the scapula along with additional superior translation of the humeral head can reduce subacromial space and result in impingement[[14](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10696154 "Alterations in shoulder kinematics and associated muscle activity in people with symptoms of shoulder impingement"), [15](http://dx.doi.org/10.2519/jospt.1999.29.10.574 "Comparison of 3-Dimensional Scapular Position and Orientation Between Subjects With and Without Shoulder Impingement"), [16](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/8816337 "Radiologic measurement of superior displacement of the humeral head in the impingement syndrome"), [17](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10717855 "Glenohumeral motion in patients with rotator cuff tears: a comparison of asymptomatic and symptomatic shoulders")].

#### **Who Usually Suffers from Subacromial Impingement?**

44% to 65% of shoulder complaints during physician visits have been reported to be due to shoulder impingement[[18](http://dx.doi.org/10.2340/16501977-0453 "Effects of physiotherapy in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: A systematic review of the literature"), [19](http://dx.doi.org/10.1093/rheumatology/keh598 "Diagnosis and relation to general health of shoulder disorders presenting to primary care"), [20](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/8546527 "Shoulder disorders in general practice: incidence, patient characteristics, and management")]. As this type of impingement is more common in overhead activities [[21](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0031-9406(05)60693-1 "The Aetiology of Subacromial Impingement Syndrome"), [22](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.pmr.2003.12.006 "Shoulder impingement syndrome")], it is also believed that those who perform manual labour and those who perform repetitive overhead tasks likely suffer from this condition[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy"), [22](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.pmr.2003.12.006 "Shoulder impingement syndrome")]. This is a common condition for patients of all ages and activity levels [[23](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2005.01.040 "Shoulder impingement syndrome")].

#### **What are the Causes, Signs and Symptoms of Subacromial Impingement?**

The causes, signs and symptoms have been grouped under one heading as there is debate as to whether the proposed causes lead to impingement or whether they are a result of the impingement. From this point on, they will be referred to as associations. The associated phenomenon of this condition have generally been categorised into two types[[5](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Abstract/1972/54010/Anterior_Acromioplasty_for_the_Chronic_Impingement.3.aspx "Anterior Acromioplasty for the Chronic Impingement Syndrome..."), [24](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21655457 "Shoulder muscle imbalance and subacromial impingement syndrome in overhead athletes")]:

1. Structural
2. Functional

There are other associations that fall outside of these two categories and are described later.

Other functional changes that lead to impingement include glenohumeral instability[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy"), [21](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0031-9406(05)60693-1 "The Aetiology of Subacromial Impingement Syndrome"), [61](http://dx.doi.org/doi:10.1016/j.arthro.2005.12.015 "Shoulder Instability and Related Rotator Cuff Tears: Arthroscopic Findings and Treatment in Patients Aged 40 to 60 Years."), [62](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2007.05.009 "A comparison of the spectrum of intra-articular lesions in acute and chronic anterior shoulder instability")], changes in strength and torque at the shoulder[[63](http://dx.doi.org/10.2486/indhealth.47.349 "Effectiveness of an at-work Exercise Program in the Prevention and Management of Neck and Low Back Complaints in Nursery School Teachers"), [64](http://dx.doi.org/10.1002/jor.20940 "Kinetic strategies of patients with shoulder impingement syndrome"), [65](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(95)80091-3 "Isokinetic evaluation of rotational strength in normal shoulders and shoulders with impingement syndrome"), [66](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2005.03.003 "Quantifying shoulder rotation weakness in patients with shoulder impingement")]. Other causes that fall outside of these two categories include eccentric overload[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy")] and pain[[5](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Abstract/1972/54010/Anterior_Acromioplasty_for_the_Chronic_Impingement.3.aspx "Anterior Acromioplasty for the Chronic Impingement Syndrome...")].

#### **How is Subacromial Impingement Diagnosed?**

There are two main motion tests to determine whether a patient suffers from this type of impingement:

1. Neer [[5](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Abstract/1972/54010/Anterior_Acromioplasty_for_the_Chronic_Impingement.3.aspx "Anterior Acromioplasty for the Chronic Impingement Syndrome..."), [12](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/6825348 "Impingement lesions")]
2. Hawkins [[67](http://www.mujofitness.com/10.1177/036354658000800302 "Impingement syndrome in athletes")]

However, there have been serious critique of these tests[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy"), [68](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2009.02.012 "Impingement mechanisms of the Neer and Hawkins signs"), [69](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/8358951 "Anatomic relationships in the shoulder impingement syndrome"), [70](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.math.2011.02.002 "Anatomical validity of the Hawkins-Kennedy test--a pilot study")], rendering them unable to diagnose the type of impingement accurately. Neither test can differentiate between subacromial impingement and internal impingement[[71](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2005.04.007 "In vivo anatomy of the Neer and Hawkins sign positions for shoulder impingement.")]. Moreover studies have shown that these tests may also cause subscapularis to compress against the bony structures[[68](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2009.02.012 "Impingement mechanisms of the Neer and Hawkins signs"), [71](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2005.04.007 "In vivo anatomy of the Neer and Hawkins sign positions for shoulder impingement.")], which is neither defined to be subacromial or internal impingement. Braman[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy")] concluded that definitive clinical tests do not exist with known anatomical basis.

### **Internal Impingement**

This type of impingement is also commonly studied but likely less so than subacromial impingement.

#### **What is Internal Impingement?**

Internal impingement is defined to be rubbing or pinching of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendon on the posterosuperior portion of the glenoid labrum[[7](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(09)80065-7 "Impingement of the deep surface of the supraspinatus tendon on the posterosuperior glenoid rim: An arthroscopic study")].

#### **When Does Internal Impingement Occur?**

This type of impingement is thought to occur during elevation of the arm whilst externally rotating[[7](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(09)80065-7 "Impingement of the deep surface of the supraspinatus tendon on the posterosuperior glenoid rim: An arthroscopic study"), [72](http://bjsm.bmj.com/content/44/5/382.abstract "Posterior superior internal impingement: an evidence-based review")]. However this type of impingement has also found to occur during the Neer and Hawkins tests[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy")].

#### **Who Usually Suffers from Internal Impingement?**

This type of impingement is usually found in overhead sport athletes[[13](http://dx.doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S36646 "Optimal management of shoulder impingement syndrome")]. This includes but is not exclusive to baseball, cricket, tennis, basketball etc. However, it is believed that normal individuals without the symptoms of a typical patient can also experience this type of impingement[[72](http://bjsm.bmj.com/content/44/5/382.abstract "Posterior superior internal impingement: an evidence-based review")].

#### **What are the Causes, Signs and Symptoms of Internal Impingement?**

All of the “associations” mentioned for subacromial impingement are also very relevant for internal impingement. This condition was first discovered with the hypothesis that repetitive throwing leads to pathological internal impingement[[7](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(09)80065-7 "Impingement of the deep surface of the supraspinatus tendon on the posterosuperior glenoid rim: An arthroscopic study")]. This is when the motion results in impingement of the rotator cuff tendon between the humeral head and the posterosuperior portion of the glenoid rim[[7](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(09)80065-7 "Impingement of the deep surface of the supraspinatus tendon on the posterosuperior glenoid rim: An arthroscopic study"), [13](http://dx.doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S36646 "Optimal management of shoulder impingement syndrome")]. It is believed that this type of impingement leads to rotator cuff tears and fraying of the glenoid labrum[[7](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(09)80065-7 "Impingement of the deep surface of the supraspinatus tendon on the posterosuperior glenoid rim: An arthroscopic study"), [38](http://dx.doi.org/10.1177/036354658501300508 "Glenoid labrum tears related to the long head of the biceps")]. It has also been hypothesised that joint laxity leads to internal impingement[[73](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/8804279 "Superior glenoid impingement. Current concepts"), [74](http://dx.doi.org/10.1177/0363546503262188 "Excessive Humeral External Rotation Results in Increased Shoulder Laxity")]. It is believed that anterior laxity of the glenohumeral capsule leads to an anterior migration of the humeral head. This results in internal impingement. Moreover, this type of instability was commonly found in overhead throwing athletes[[75](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/4091915 "Arthroscopy of the shoulder in the management of partial tears of the rotator cuff: a preliminary report"), [76](http://dx.doi.org/10.1177/036354659702500504 "Shoulder motion and laxity in the professional baseball player")] and the main associated condition[[77](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10627343 "Arthroscopic findings in the overhand throwing athlete: evidence for posterior internal impingement of the rotator cuff.")].

The hypothesis that instability leads to internal impingement has been challenged. Burkhart[[78](http://journals.lww.com/shoulderelbowsurgery/Fulltext/2001/06000/Dead_Arm_Syndrome__Torsional_SLAP_Lesions_versus.2.aspx "Dead Arm Syndrome: Torsional SLAP Lesions versus Internal Impingement."), [79](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16958436 "Internal impingement of the shoulder")] believed that internal impingement was not due to a pathological cause, rather it is a natural phenomenon to prevent hyper-external rotation of the glenohumeral joint. He believed that a tight posterior capsule resulted in internal rotation deficit. This then leads to a shift in the glenohumeral contact point in the posterior and superior direction. This is said to decrease the ‘cam effect’ and results in a secondary stretching of the anterior glenohumeral capsule leading to an increase in external rotation. The increase in external rotation is what causes SLAP tears. He stated that the rotator cuff and labrum injuries are not due to the abrasion, rather it is due to the increased shear forces due to the twisting mechanism whilst throwing.

One of the main symptoms of internal impingement is pain in the posterior portion of the shoulder[[72](http://bjsm.bmj.com/content/44/5/382.abstract "Posterior superior internal impingement: an evidence-based review")]. Overhead athletes tend to complain about pain during the late stage of the cocking-phase of throwing and that it can be difficult to warm up[[72](http://bjsm.bmj.com/content/44/5/382.abstract "Posterior superior internal impingement: an evidence-based review")]. Some patients have also reported to experience anterior shoulder pain[[72](http://bjsm.bmj.com/content/44/5/382.abstract "Posterior superior internal impingement: an evidence-based review")].

Some of the signs of internal impingement include the same amount of loss of internal rotation as increase in external rotation[[80](http://dx.doi.org/10.1177/0363546505281804 "Glenohumeral Range of Motion Deficits and Posterior Shoulder Tightness in Throwers With Pathologic Internal Impingement")]. Patients may also show a loss of internal rotation during 90° of abduction[[79](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16958436 "Internal impingement of the shoulder")].

#### **How is Internal Impingement Diagnosed?**

A clinician may perform range of motion tests to check for some of the signs mentioned above. The Jobe relocation test similar to the Neer and Hawkins test is often positive for internal impingement and has a sensitivity of approximately 75%[[76](http://dx.doi.org/10.1177/036354659702500504 "Shoulder motion and laxity in the professional baseball player")]. During this test the patient is laid down facing up. The arm is then abducted to 90° and externally rotated. The clinician then applies an anterior force to the shoulder followed by a posterior force. The test is considered positive if the patient feels posterior pain when the anterior force is applied and relieved of pain when a posterior force is applied[[72](http://bjsm.bmj.com/content/44/5/382.abstract "Posterior superior internal impingement: an evidence-based review")].

### **Coracoid Impingement**

Coracoid impingement is less common in comparison to subacromial and internal impingements[[11](http://10.0.3.239/s11517-013-1074-1 "Shoulder impingement revisited: evolution of diagnostic understanding in orthopedic surgery and physical therapy"), [81](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10226964 "Rotator interval lesions and their relation to coracoid impingement syndrome")].

#### **What is Coracoid Impingement?**

This type of impingement is defined to be the collision and compression of the subscapularis tendon between the coracoid process and the lesser tuberosity of the humerus during motion[[8](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Citation/1909/06040/AN_ANATOMIC_AND_MECHANICAL_STUDY_OF_THE.1.aspx "AN ANATOMIC AND MECHANICAL STUDY OF THE SHOULDER-JOINT, EXPL..."), [82](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/4055864 "The role of the coracoid process in the chronic impingement syndrome")]. It has also been referred to as “subcoracoid impingement syndrome”.

#### **When Does Coracoid Impingement Occur?**

This type of impingement usually occurs during a combination of flexion, adduction and internal rotation of the humerus after a history of repetition of these motions, overuse and micro-trauma[[83](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s12178-009-9044-9 "Coracoid impingement syndrome: a literature review")].

#### **Who Usually Suffers from Coracoid Impingement?**

There are no reports in the published literature of the incidence and prevalence of this condition as of yet[[83](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s12178-009-9044-9 "Coracoid impingement syndrome: a literature review")]. However, an individual case study may suggest that rock-climbers may be more prone to this type of impingement[[84](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.wem.2010.12.005 "Coracoid Impingement Syndrome Due to Intensive Rock Climbing Training")].

#### **What are the Causes, Signs and Symptoms of Coracoid Impingement?**

One of the main causes of coracoid impingement is overuse and micro-trauma of the shoulder. The following table categorises the additional possible causes[[82](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/4055864 "The role of the coracoid process in the chronic impingement syndrome"), [85](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/1783548 "The subcoracoid impingement syndrome: clinical, semeiologic and therapeutic considerations"), [86](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16369628 "Old unreduced posterior sternoclavicular dislocation and coracoid impingement")] into three subsets[[83](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s12178-009-9044-9 "Coracoid impingement syndrome: a literature review")]:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | **Cause** |
| **Idiopathic** | Malposition of the coracoid tip  Calcification of the coracoid tip and subscapularis tendon |
| **Traumatic** | Humeral head and neck fracture  Malunion of coracoid fracture  Malunion of glenoid fracture  Displaced fractures of scapular neck  Posterior sternoclavicular dislocation |
| **Iatrogenic** | Previous anterior shoulder surgery (Bristow/Trillat procedure)  Posterior glenoid osteotomy  Acromionectomy |

Patients can suffer from tendinosis and pain inside of the shoulder. The continuous contact between the coracoid and the lesser tuberosity causes progressive bone degeneration, inflammation of the subscapularis bursa and damage to the subscapularis tendon. This type of impingement can lead to subscapularis partial or full thickness tear[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes"), [83](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s12178-009-9044-9 "Coracoid impingement syndrome: a literature review")].

#### **How is Coracoid Impingement Diagnosed?**

The diagnosis of coracoid impingement has not been exclusively studied in-depth and remains a condition that is diagnosed by eliminating other possible conditions[[83](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s12178-009-9044-9 "Coracoid impingement syndrome: a literature review")]. However, there are tests that have the potential to indicate the presence of this type of impingement. The ‘coracoid impingement test’ is similar to the Hawkins test, except that the patient’s arm is placed in adduction, forward flexion and internal rotation to try and induce contact between the lesser tuberosity and the coracoid. The test would be deemed positive if the patient feels pain[[87](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/2312576 "The coracoid impingement syndrome")]. Patients usually feel pain during mid-range of elevation in comparison to end-range in those with subacromial impingement syndrome[[82](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/4055864 "The role of the coracoid process in the chronic impingement syndrome")].

Some clinicians may inject a local anaesthetic to determine whether there is relief of pain[[87](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/2312576 "The coracoid impingement syndrome")]. If there is relief of pain, it is believed that the patient suffers from this type of impingement. However, the accuracy and validity of this test has been called into question[[88](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16958434 "Clinical evaluation of impingement: what to do and what works")].

Radiology (X-rays and MRI scans) have been used to try to detect and diagnose this type of impingement, however the accuracy and specificity is questionable[[83](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s12178-009-9044-9 "Coracoid impingement syndrome: a literature review")].

### **Suprascapular Nerve Impingement**

This type of impingement is rarely mentioned as one of the shoulder impingement conditions and was cited to be the fourth type by Rossi, 1998[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes")]. This could be because this type of impingement is not related to compression of a muscle or tendon between or against bony structures.

#### **What is Suprascapular Nerve Impingement?**

As the name suggests this condition is the entrapment of the suprascapular nerve. The entrapment could occur at the suprascapular notch[[89](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/3408338 "Painless infraspinatus atrophy due to suprascapular nerve entrapment")] or the spinoglenoid notch[[90](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/7204456 "Suprascapular-nerve entrapment")]. Damage to this nerve could then lead to infraspinatus and supraspinatus paralysis and atrophy[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes"), [91](http://dx.doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.34.3.174 "Suprascapular neuropathy in volleyball players")].

#### **When Does Suprascapular Nerve Impingement Occur?**

It is suggested that this type of impingement occurs during extreme motions such as during service of the ball in volleyball[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes")]. In addition, elongation and irritation of the nerve has been shown to occur at extreme arm positions in a cadaver study[[92](http://www.mujofitness.com/ShoulderImpingement#ref92 "Suprascapular nerve strain; a biomechanical study")].

#### **Who Usually Suffers from Suprascapular Nerve Impingement?**

This condition has primarily been found in volleyball players[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes"), [91](http://dx.doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.34.3.174 "Suprascapular neuropathy in volleyball players"), [93](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/3805088 "Suprascapular neuropathy in volleyball players"), [94](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(98)90205-1 "Suprascapular nerve rotator cuff compression syndrome in volleyball players")] with a reported incidence rate of 20%[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes")]. There are no incidence and prevalence reports on the general population.

#### **What are the Causes, Signs and Symptoms of Suprascapular Nerve Impingement?**

There are a few hypotheses that describe the causes of this condition. Some believe it is a result of trauma[[95](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/1199714 "Fracture of the scapular notch associated with lesion of the suprascapular nerve"), [96](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/6712457 "Rotator cuff rupture: management with suprascapular neuropathy")], hypertrophy of the spinoglenoid ligament[[97](http://dx.doi.org/10.1002/ana.410120320 "Entrapment of the suprascapular nerve at the spinoglenoid notch"), [98](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/3185844 "[Chronic neuropathy of the suprascapular nerve in high performance athletes]")], strain[[99](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/3566518 "Suprascapular neuropathy after intensive progressive resistive exercise: case report"), [100](http://dx.doi.org/10.1177/036354659001800113 "Suprascapular neuropathy in pitchers")], ganglion cysts[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes"), [101](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/1985986 "Entrapment neuropathy of the suprascapular nerve by a ganglion. A report of three cases")] and the repetition of volleyball serves lead to gradual degeneration and elongation of the nerve at the notch.

Damage and entrapment of the nerve results in the following signs and symptoms[[1](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S0720-048X(98)00042-4 "Shoulder impingement syndromes"), [91](http://dx.doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.34.3.174 "Suprascapular neuropathy in volleyball players"), [92](http://www.mujofitness.com/AdminArea/Page/Edit/4065#ref92 "Suprascapular nerve strain; a biomechanical study")]:

|  |
| --- |
| **Signs & Symptoms** |
| Infraspinatus atrophy |
| Decreased strength in external rotation |
| Pain in posterior and lateral part of the dominant arm |
| Increased range of motion |

#### **How is Suprascapular Nerve Impingement Diagnosed?**

Motion tests have not been developed to detect this condition and is likely diagnosed by eliminating other possibilities. EMG analysis may be performed to detect denervation and electrical activity in the muscle.

If muscle atrophy has started, a physical examination of the infraspinatus could indicate a positive diagnosis of the condition.

## **What are the Shoulder Impingement Treatment Options?**

There are specific treatments for each of the different types of impingement, however they can all be brought together under the following categories:

- Non-invasive
- Acupuncture
- Injections
- Surgery

This section will describe each of the different treatment types and the overall message that the literature concludes.

### **Non-Invasive Treatment Options for Shoulder Impingement**

There are numerous treatment options, which fall under one or more of the following types:

- Spine manipulation
- Physiotherapy (includes mobilisation with movement).
- Manual Therapy
- Taping
- Other

In any case, conservative or non-invasive treatment options are highly recommended in comparison to others[[32](http://www.mujofitness.com/AdminArea/Page/Edit/4065#ref32 "Conservative treatment preferences and the plausible mechanism of Neer’s stage 1 of shoulder impingement in younger people")]. Moreover, if these are done properly surgery can be avoided[[102](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21648167 "[Implementation of preoperative physiotherapy and recovery after shoulder impingement surgery]")].

#### **Spine Manipulation**

Spine manipulation or otherwise known as thoracic spine thrust usually involves the bony re-positioning of the vertebrae. The treatment may also include localised massage therapy. This treatment option has its support as it has shown to work[[103](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.math.2008.05.005 "The short-term effects of thoracic spine thrust manipulation on patients with shoulder impingement syndrome")] and reduce pain[[104](http://dx.doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2014.4760 "Scapular Kinematics Pre– and Post–Thoracic Thrust Manipulation in Individuals With and Without Shoulder Impingement Symptoms: A Randomized Controlled Study")]. Others have recommended that this treatment is combined with some form of exercise, ideally supervised physiotherapy[[105](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jcm.2016.10.002 "A Preliminary Randomized Clinical Trial on the Effect of Cervicothoracic Manipulation Plus Supervised Exercises vs a Home Exercise Program for the Treatment of Shoulder Impingement")]. Some clinicians may perform cervical spine manipulation instead of and along with thoracic spine manipulation, however Cook argues that there is no difference between the two and that both provide the same benefit[[106](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.math.2013.05.007 "The addition of cervical unilateral posterior–anterior mobilisation in the treatment of patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: A randomised clinical trial")].

More high quality research is needed to conclude whether this treatment is truly beneficial. However, there have been no major findings or studies to suggest that this treatment can do harm.

#### **Physiotherapy**

This is by far the most studied type of treatment in literature in comparison to the others. This is probably due to the numerous different treatment methods and protocols. Physiotherapy is believed to be extremely important in the treatment of shoulder impingement[[107](http://dx.doi.org/10.2522/ptj.20150427 "Physical Therapists’ Perceptions and Use of Exercise in the Management of Subacromial Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: Focus Group Study")] and a 12 week rehab program has proven to work in baseball players[[108](http://dx.doi.org/10.12965/jer.140087 "A 12-week rehabilitation program improves body composition, pain sensation, and internal/external torques of baseball pitchers with shoulder impingement symptom")]. The types of physiotherapy can further be broken down into the following categories:

- Strengthening or resistance based exercises
- Stretching
- Neuromuscular control
- Other

In addition, each of these categories of treatment methods can either be supervised or self/home-based and can be combined with one another. Both of which have been shown to be as beneficial as each other[[109](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.math.2015.03.013 "Is exercise effective for the management of subacromial impingement syndrome and other soft tissue injuries of the shoulder? A systematic review by the Ontario Protocol for Traffic Injury Management (OPTIMa) Collaboration")] and shown to complement each other[[110](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2009.10.005 "The effects of unsupervised movement training with visual feedback on upper limb kinematic in persons with shoulder impingement syndrome")].

Exercise in general has been found to benefit those who suffer from shoulder impingement[[108](http://dx.doi.org/10.12965/jer.140087 "A 12-week rehabilitation program improves body composition, pain sensation, and internal/external torques of baseball pitchers with shoulder impingement symptom"), [111](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2013.09.022 "Pulsed Electromagnetic Field and Exercises in Patients With Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Clinical Trial"), [112](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.circen.2016.04.005 "Effectiveness of physiotherapy on painful shoulder impingement syndrome")]. One study showed that in just 9 sessions patients showed a 90% improvement in function[[112](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.circen.2016.04.005 "Effectiveness of physiotherapy on painful shoulder impingement syndrome")]. In addition, Dutch physios who use evidence based treatment methods use exercises to treat shoulder impingement[[113](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jmpt.2012.10.009 "Evidence-Based Treatment Methods for the Management of Shoulder Impingement Syndrome Among Dutch-Speaking Physiotherapists: An Online, Web-Based Survey")].

Numerous studies have investigated the efficacy of strengthening and resistance based exercises[[114](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.rehab.2016.10.004 "Structured Wii protocol for rehabilitation of shoulder impingement syndrome: A pilot study"), [115](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2015.06.007 "Effectiveness of the eccentric exercise therapy in physically active adults with symptomatic shoulder impingement or lateral epicondylar tendinopathy: A systematic review"), [116](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/22441232 "Eccentric training for shoulder abductors improves pain, function and isokinetic performance in subjects with shoulder impingement syndrome: a case series"), [117](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.physio.2015.03.3705 "Is rotator cuff strengthening necessary in a shoulder impingement exercise program?"), [118](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24050647 "Muscle shortening manoeuvre reduces pain and functional impairment in shoulder impingement syndrome: clinical and ultrasonographic evidence"), [119](http://dx.doi.org/10.1002/jor.21300 "Adaptive patterns of movement during arm elevation test in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome"), [120](http://dx.doi.org/10.1002/art.23576 "Progressive resistance training in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: A randomized controlled trial"), [121](http://dx.doi.org/10.2519/jospt.1993.17.5.212 "Nonoperative Management of Secondary Shoulder Impingement Syndrome"), [122](http://dx.doi.org/10.4238/2014.January.31.1 "Effect of isokinetic training on shoulder impingement")]. Majority of these studies are in support of these types of exercises. However, some research suggests that rotator cuff strengthening exercises are not beneficial because the rotator cuff strength was found to be the same between patients with shoulder impingement and normal individuals[[117](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.physio.2015.03.3705 "Is rotator cuff strengthening necessary in a shoulder impingement exercise program?")].

Eccentric loading exercises on the other hand, have shown to be beneficial and recommended[[115](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2015.06.007 "Effectiveness of the eccentric exercise therapy in physically active adults with symptomatic shoulder impingement or lateral epicondylar tendinopathy: A systematic review"), [116](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/22441232 "Eccentric training for shoulder abductors improves pain, function and isokinetic performance in subjects with shoulder impingement syndrome: a case series")]. Similarly, strengthening exercises that revolve around muscle shortening have been shown to work as well[[118](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24050647 "Muscle shortening manoeuvre reduces pain and functional impairment in shoulder impingement syndrome: clinical and ultrasonographic evidence")]. Lin et al. suggested that strengthening the lower-trapezius and the serratus anterior could help restore normal scapular kinematics to prevent and alleviate impingement[[119](http://dx.doi.org/10.1002/jor.21300 "Adaptive patterns of movement during arm elevation test in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome")]. Furthermore, Kamkar et al. stated that individualised scapulothoracic strengthening should be part of any exercise program[[121](http://dx.doi.org/10.2519/jospt.1993.17.5.212 "Nonoperative Management of Secondary Shoulder Impingement Syndrome")]. Moreover, Kromer et al. found that exercises work when they are individualised to the patient[[123](http://dx.doi.org/10.2340/16501977-1142 "Physiotherapy in patients with clinical signs of shoulder impingement syndrome: A randomized controlled trial")].

Stretching of the shoulder girdle was found to be beneficial and should be added to other exercise or treatment methods[[124](http://dx.doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2016-0182 "Stretching Exercises for Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: Effects of 6-Week Program on Shoulder Tightness, Pain and Disability Status")].

Dong et al. advised that exercise as a treatment method is best when the syndrome is diagnosed early. However, they also state that in severe cases, exercises should be prescribed first as it has similar outcomes to surgery[[125](http://dx.doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000000510 "Treatments for Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: a PRISMA systematic review and network meta-analysis")].

Skolimowski et al. went further and recommended that pain should be treated first and then the aim should be to restore function[[126](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19043356 "Principles of physiotherapeutic management of shoulder impingement syndrome")].

Neuromuscular control has proven to be a large topic as a treatment type for shoulder impingement. The main purpose of neuromuscular control is to restore normal muscle activation patterns. It was already shown earlier that patients with shoulder impingement have different muscle activation and recruitment patterns. Moreover, multiple related studies show that neuromuscular control or motor learning works[[110](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2009.10.005 "The effects of unsupervised movement training with visual feedback on upper limb kinematic in persons with shoulder impingement syndrome"), [127](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ptsp.2006.09.004 "The effects of a six week scapular muscle exercise programme on the muscle activity of the scapular rotators in tennis players with shoulder impingement—A pilot study"), [128](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2012.06.010 "Motor control retraining exercises for shoulder impingement: effects on function, muscle activation, and biomechanics in young adults"), [129](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24429918 "Neurocognitive therapeutic exercise improves pain and function in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: a single-blind randomized controlled clinical trial"), [130](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2012.09.007 "EMG biofeedback effectiveness to alter muscle activity pattern and scapular kinematics in subjects with and without shoulder impingement"), [131](http://dx.doi.org/10.3109/09638288.2013.833309 "The therapeutic role of motor imagery on the functional rehabilitation of a stage II shoulder impingement syndrome")]. It can help with pain, increase mobility[[131](http://dx.doi.org/10.3109/09638288.2013.833309 "The therapeutic role of motor imagery on the functional rehabilitation of a stage II shoulder impingement syndrome"), [132](http://dx.doi.org/0.1589/jpts.26.1803 "Soft Tissue Mobilization and PNF Improve Range of Motion and Minimize Pain Level in Shoulder Impingement")] and stabilise the shoulder[[133](http://dx.doi.org/10.1589/jpts.25.1359 "Effects of Shoulder Stabilization Exercise on Pain and Functional Recovery of Shoulder Impingement Syndrome Patients")]. Some have found it to be better than conventional or traditional physio and manual therapy[[134](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2016.01.040 "Serial Comparison of Clinical Outcomes After Arthroscopic Capsular Release for Refractory Frozen Shoulder With and Without Diabetes"), [135](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25664288 "The effects of scapular stabilization based exercise therapy on pain, posture, flexibility and shoulder mobility in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: a controlled randomized clinical trial"), [136](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2015.10.015 "Advanced glycation end products in idiopathic frozen shoulders")]. Dajah et al. recommended the use of massage therapy and both internal and external proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) exercises to decrease pain and increase range of motion[[132](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4242960/ "Soft Tissue Mobilization and PNF Improve Range of Motion and Minimize Pain Level in Shoulder Impingement")].

#### **Manual Therapy**

Manual therapy is defined to be the “Application of an accurately determined and specifically directed manual force to the body, in order to improve mobility in areas that are restricted; in joints, in connective tissues or in skeletal muscles”[[137](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manual_therapy "Manual Therapy - Wikipedia")]. In the context of shoulder impingement, the types of manual therapy include massage and mobilisation techniques. Trigger point therapy in particular has been shown to help with pain[[138](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jbmt.2010.12.003 "Changes in pain and pressure pain sensitivity after manual treatment of active trigger points in patients with unilateral shoulder impingement: A case series")]. Similarly, mobilisation with movement was shown to work[[139](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jmpt.2014.12.008 "Effects of Mobilization With Movement on Pain and Range of Motion in Patients With Unilateral Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: A Randomized Controlled Trial")].

Many studies recommend combining manual therapy with some form of exercise[[140](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.physio.2016.10.067 "The adjunctive benefit of manual therapy in addition to therapeutic exercise for shoulder impingement syndrome: a systematic review"), [141](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s00167-007-0288-x "Comparison of conservative treatment with and without manual physical therapy for patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: a prospective, randomized clinical trial"), [142](http://dx.doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2000.30.3.126 "Comparison of Supervised Exercise With and Without Manual Physical Therapy for Patients With Shoulder Impingement Syndrome")]. There are some who argue that manual therapy may not necessarily be a beneficial treatment option[[143](http://dx.doi.org/10.2340/16501977-1867 "Effectiveness of physiotherapy and costs in patients with clinical signs of shoulder impingement syndrome: One-year follow-up of a randomized controlled trial"), [144](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jmpt.2016.08.001 "Immediate Effects of Mobilization With Movement vs Sham Technique on Range of Motion, Strength, and Function in Patients With Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: Randomized Clinical Trial")].

#### **Taping**

Taping or also known as kinesiology taping refers to the use of tape to lift the skin away from the soft tissues. The purpose of this technique is to provide support and encourage better kinematics and thus dynamics without restricting the range of motion.

There is some controversy as to whether this type of treatment works or provides any benefit at all. Some studies suggest that it either doesn’t work or provides pain relief for a very short period i.e. 1 week or is very limited in its benefits[[145](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ptsp.2016.06.006 "Kinesiology taping does not alter shoulder strength, shoulder proprioception, or scapular kinematics in healthy, physically active subjects and subjects with Subacromial Impingement Syndrome") ,[146](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24377066 "Clinical effectiveness of kinesiological taping on pain and pain-free shoulder range of motion in patients with shoulder impingement syndrome: a randomized, double blinded, placebo-controlled trial"), [147](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2014.07.011 "Rigid and Elastic taping changes scapular kinematics and pain in subjects with shoulder impingement syndrome; an experimental study")]. Another few suggest that it can work but only in the short term[[148](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s10067-010-1475-6 "Kinesio taping compared to physical therapy modalities for the treatment of shoulder impingement syndrome"), [149](http://dx.doi.org/10.1179/jmt.2009.17.1.6E "Does Scapula Taping Facilitate Recovery for Shoulder Impingement Symptoms? A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial")]. Selkowitz et al. found that muscle activity in the upper and lower trapezius had changed[[150](http://dx.doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2007.2467 "The Effects of Scapular Taping on the Surface Electromyographic Signal Amplitude of Shoulder Girdle Muscles During Upper Extremity Elevation in Individuals With Suspected Shoulder Impingement Syndrome")], suggesting that taping could have its benefits.

Higher quality studies are required and specific protocols need to be developed to determine whether taping really does provide benefit for shoulder impingement patients.

#### **Other**

There are numerous other treatment methods, which are mostly used in combination with one of the above. Some of these include radiotherapy (only works for secondary impingement[[151](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.eujim.2009.08.123 "Resistance in therapy of the functional shoulder impingement syndrome—Pathogenesis and possibilities of treatment in complementary medicine")]), HLT patches[[152](http://dx.doi.org/10.2147/JPR.S63118 "A randomized clinical study of the heated lidocaine/tetracaine patch versus subacromial corticosteroid injection for the treatment of pain associated with shoulder impingement syndrome")], hot and cold compress[[112](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.circen.2016.04.005 "Effectiveness of physiotherapy on painful shoulder impingement syndrome")], herbal remedies, and simply an improvement in dietary intake. The latter are aimed to better health at the cellular level and improve blood flow.

### **Acupuncture**

This type of treatment has recently been shown to be safe and provide benefit[[153](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2016.01.003 "Acupuncture treatment of shoulder impingement syndrome: A randomized controlled trial")]. However, this type of treatment is in its early stages of research. More randomised control trials are needed to conclude with confidence that acupuncture works.

#### **Injections**

There are a few different types of injections. The most popular is the corticosteroid injection. Other types and protocols include sclerosing polidocanol injections[[154](http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s00167-006-0205-8 "Sclerosing polidocanol injections to treat chronic painful shoulder impingement syndrome-results of a two-centre collaborative pilot study")] and mesotherapy[[155](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.rehab.2013.07.1057 "Effects of the mesotherapy on the mobility of the shoulder in the impingement syndrome by tendinitis of supra spinatus over 54 cases")].

The use of corticosteroid injections for treating shoulder impingement is controversial with many claiming that it either doesn’t work, has no added benefit to conservative treatments, or had the worst outcomes compared to other treatment methods[[125](http://dx.doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000000510 "Treatments for Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: a PRISMA systematic review and network meta-analysis"), [152](http://dx.doi.org/10.2147/JPR.S63118 "A randomized clinical study of the heated lidocaine/tetracaine patch versus subacromial corticosteroid injection for the treatment of pain associated with shoulder impingement syndrome"), [156](http://dx.doi.org/10.7326/M13-2199 "One-Year Outcome of Subacromial Corticosteroid Injection Compared With Manual Physical Therapy for the Management of the Unilateral Shoulder Impingement Syndrome"), [157](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.physio.2015.03.1190 "One-year outcome of subacromial corticosteroid injection versus manual physical therapy for unilateral shoulder impingement syndrome: a pragmatic randomized clinical trial"), [158](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jpain.2014.01.025 "The pain quality response profile of a corticosteroid injections and heated lidocaine/tetracaine patch in the treatment of shoulder impingement syndrome"), [159](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/18309381 "Quantification of two-dimensional glenohumeral rhythm in persons with and without symptoms of shoulder impingement")]. Some researchers suggest that it has some benefit in those patients who also have supraspinatus tears[[155](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.rehab.2013.07.1057 "Effects of the mesotherapy on the mobility of the shoulder in the impingement syndrome by tendinitis of supra spinatus over 54 cases")]. Akram et al. also found that this type of injections works in those who do not have rotator cuff tears but do have a curved acromion[[28](http://www.mujofitness.com/ShoulderImpingement#ref28 "Acromion Types and Role of Corticosteroid with Shoulder Impingement Syndrome")]. Those who do suggest that these injections work also state that it should be used in combination with physiotherapy[[125](http://dx.doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000000510 "Treatments for Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: a PRISMA systematic review and network meta-analysis"), [160](http://dx.doi.org.uk/10.11138/mltj/2015.5.3.195 "High volume image guided injections and structured rehabilitation in shoulder impingement syndrome: a retrospective study")]. In addition, it is best for short-term relief, whereas physiotherapy is better suited for long-term results[[160](http://dx.doi.org.uk/10.11138/mltj/2015.5.3.195 "High volume image guided injections and structured rehabilitation in shoulder impingement syndrome: a retrospective study")].

### **Surgery**

The main surgical option to treat shoulder impingement is subacromial decompression, also known as acromioplasty. The intent behind this treatment is to relieve the pressure in the subacromial space by shaving away the underside of the acromion. Neer[[5](http://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/Abstract/1972/54010/Anterior_Acromioplasty_for_the_Chronic_Impingement.3.aspx "Anterior Acromioplasty for the Chronic Impingement Syndrome...")] hypothesised that subacromial impingement syndrome was due to an abnormal acromion shape and thus impingement was purely due to a mechanical cause. According to his theory, any patient with shoulder impingement syndrome would directly and immediately benefit from acromioplasty. However this is not the case as some have required further surgery or continued to suffer from pain[[161](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2007.02.116 "Anterior acromioplasty for the shoulder impingement syndrome: Long-term outcome"), [162](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/2225624 "Anterior acromioplasty for treatment of the shoulder impingement syndrome")].

Some of the most common conclusions from literature are that there is no difference in outcomes between surgery and exercise[[163](http://dx.doi.org/10.1302/2046-3758.27.2000163 "No evidence of long-term benefits of arthroscopicacromioplasty in the treatment of shoulder impingement syndrome: Five-year results of a randomised controlled trial"), [164](http://dx.doi.org/10.1302/0301-620X.91B10.22094 "Does arthroscopic acromioplasty provide any additional value in the treatment of shoulder impingement syndrome?: A TWO-YEAR RANDOMISED CONTROLLED TRIAL")]. An in-depth review of all trials of the surgery published in 2015 concluded that surgery is not more effect than conservative treatment[[165](http://dx.doi.org/10.3109/09638288.2014.907364 "Conservative treatment or surgery for shoulder impingement: systematic review and meta-analysis")].

Some of those authors who suggest that surgery does work provide some caveats. Khare et al. suggest that it is good for those who failed to recover using conservative treatment, however the patients in their study received both glenohumeral arthroscopy and subacromial decompression[[166](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jajs.2015.11.004 "Clinical and radiological outcomes of arthroscopic subacromial decompression for stage-II impingement of shoulder")]. Ketola et al. on the other hand, suggest that if exercise fails to provide benefit then surgery should not be performed[[167](http://dx.doi.org/10.3109/17453674.2015.1033309 "Which patients do not recover from shoulder impingement syndrome, either with operative treatment or with nonoperative treatment?")]. Others state specific criteria on how to choose patients who will benefit from the surgery[[168](http://dx.doi.org/10.1302/0301-620X.94B8.29001 "Arthroscopic subacromial decompression is effective in selected patients with shoulder impingement syndrome"), [169](http://dx.doi.org/10.3109/17453674.2011.623571 "Patients with shoulder impingement remain satisfied 6 years after arthroscopic subacromial decompression"), [170](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/S1058-2746(09)80002-5 "The shoulder impingement syndrome: The results of surgical decompression")]. A handful of studies suggest that surgery does work without stating whether patients need to be chosen appropriately[[171](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2008.04.073 "Long-term Outcomes of Arthroscopic Acromioplasty for Chronic Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: A Prospective Cohort Study With a Minimum of 12 Years’ Follow-up"), [172](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/?term=19811715 "Shoulder impingement syndrome: outcome of arthroscopic subacromial decompression"), [173](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ics.2004.03.276 "CT-guided subabcromial soft tissue decompression for impingement syndrome of the shoulder"), [174](http://www.mujofitness.com/ShoulderImpingement#ref174 "Subacromial decompression for shoulder impingement syndrome")]. One study concluded that surgery showed benefits after a 12 year follow up[[171](http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2008.04.073 "Long-term Outcomes of Arthroscopic Acromioplasty for Chronic Shoulder Impingement Syndrome: A Prospective Cohort Study With a Minimum of 12 Years’ Follow-up")]. Although, these studies showed benefit, they did not conclude that it was a better treatment option in comparison to physiotherapy or exercise.
